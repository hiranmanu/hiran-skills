# Company Research Methodology

Source order and what to extract at each depth. Depth is set by the speed
mode in `ip-config.md` — Rapid stops after §2 (site + news only), Standard
covers §§1-6 (site through employer/team research), Deep adds the
adjacent-industry playbook translation in §5.

**Don't stop at site + news.** The single most common research gap is
treating the company's own site and a general news search as sufficient.
For anything Standard or above, the highest-signal sources are often the
ones a candidate doesn't think to check: filings registries (real
financial/ownership fact vs. inference), what the company is actually
hiring for right now, and what its own team's LinkedIn profiles say about
its real maturity. See steps 2.7, 5.5, and 6 below.

## 0. Entity disambiguation (standing rule, every mode)

Before citing any figure, client, or fact "about" the company, check
whether the name maps to more than one real entity — a similarly-branded
sister company, a spun-off division, a franchise, or a rebrand. This is
not a hypothetical: it has caught a real error before (a UniLED
interview-prep session initially attributed a hardware/ops product line
to the software company being interviewed with, because both trade under
near-identical branding with a shared founder history). Signs to check:
two near-identical domains, two LinkedIn company pages, a "which
[Company] do you mean" moment in search results, or a JD/recruiter brief
that describes a narrower scope than the company's public site does.
If ambiguity turns up, resolve it explicitly (different leadership,
different address, different registered entity) before building anything
on top of it, and note the resolution in the dossier so it doesn't get
re-litigated in a later round.

## 1. Company site (always, every mode)

- Newsroom / press releases page — most recent 2-3 items.
- "About" / investor relations page if public — gives the official
  business-model framing in their own words (useful for mirroring language
  in the intro and smart questions).
- Careers/culture page if present — values language often gets echoed back
  by HR screeners; worth noting even if it reads as boilerplate.

## 2. Recent internet news (always, every mode)

Search for `"{Company}" news` and `"{Company}" 2026` (or current year).
Prioritize:
- Funding rounds, M&A activity, leadership changes (last 6 months)
- Product launches or pivots
- Layoffs, controversies, or regulatory issues — know these before walking
  in; don't bring them up unprompted, but don't be blindsided if asked.

Note publication date on everything — stale news read as current is worse
than no news.

**Partnership-cadence timeline (Standard + Deep).** Don't just list news
items — order every named partnership, hire, or announcement from the
last 6-12 months by date and look for the implicit story the cadence
tells (e.g. "one partner announcement every 3-4 weeks, all US-facing" —
that's a deliberate push, not a string of coincidences, and naming the
pattern itself is stronger prep than naming any single item in it).

## 2.5 Named-interviewer research (Standard + Deep, always if names are known)

Research the *people*, not just the company — separately from the
company-level research above:
- LinkedIn: current title, tenure, what they owned before this company.
- Anything they personally authored or said publicly — a byline article,
  a podcast appearance, a conference talk, a quote in press coverage.
  This is higher-value than company news: citing an interviewer's own
  stated thesis back to them (accurately, not flattering-for-its-own-sake)
  is the single highest-signal "I did real homework" move available, and
  it tells you what they actually care about better than a JD does.
- Any industry-body role, advisory position, or outside credibility marker
  (standards committees, working groups) — often absent from the JD
  entirely but genuinely differentiating context.
- If multiple interviewers are confirmed for a round with different
  backgrounds (e.g. a commercial exec vs. a technical co-founder), note
  which questions/stories are likely to land with which person — see
  `ip-question-bank.md` for tiering smart questions by audience.

## 2.6 Hiring signals (Standard + Deep)

Check the company's live job postings (their own careers page and/or
LinkedIn Jobs) — what a company is *actually* hiring for right now is a
sharper, harder-to-fake signal of real priorities than any mission
statement or press release. Look for: how many open roles sit in the same
function as this one (a lone opening vs. a team being built out), what
seniority is being hired around this role (a signal of whether it reports
into a gap or into an already-staffed layer), and any pattern across
functions (e.g. a wave of US-market roles alongside a UK-only product
team) that corroborates or contradicts the partnership-cadence read above.

## 2.7 Company filings / registries (Standard + Deep, especially for
private/unlisted companies)

Don't leave ownership, funding, and financials as inference from press
coverage alone when a primary-source registry exists and is often free to
check:
- **UK:** Companies House — filed accounts, the Persons with Significant
  Control (PSC) register (real ownership, not a guess), confirmation
  statements, director appointment history, charges/mortgages (debt).
- **US:** SEC EDGAR for public companies; state Secretary of State
  filings for private ones (thinner, but confirms registered status/
  officers).
- **Other jurisdictions / cross-border:** OpenCorporates as a general
  index; Crunchbase/PitchBook for funding history if accessible.

This turns "no evidence of institutional funding found" (a web-search
inference) into "confirmed founder-owned, no charges on record, PSC is
[name]" (a sourced fact) — materially stronger for the dossier's company
snapshot and for anything in the "verify before you say this" list.

## 3. How they make money (Standard + Deep)

Identify: primary revenue model (subscription, transaction/take-rate,
advertising, licensing, services), customer segment (B2B/B2C/B2B2C), and
where this role sits relative to the revenue engine — is it a cost center,
a growth lever, or infrastructure for one? This framing directly shapes
which STAR stories and smart questions land in Phase 3/4.

## 4. Annual reports & earnings calls (Standard + Deep)

- **Public company:** most recent annual report (10-K/annual report) and
  the last 1-2 quarterly earnings call transcripts/summaries. Pull: stated
  strategic priorities, any explicit mention of the function/team this
  role sits in, and forward guidance language (what they're telling
  investors is coming next — this is gold for smart questions).
- **Private/PE-backed:** no public filings; substitute recent funding
  announcement (round size, lead investor, stated use of funds) and any
  founder/exec interviews or podcast appearances.
- **Deep mode only:** compare current vs. prior year/quarter to spot
  trend direction (growing/shrinking a function, new market entry,
  cost discipline language) — this is what makes a question sound like
  genuine homework rather than a generic "tell me about the company"
  fish.

## 5. Competitive landscape (Deep only)

1-2 named competitors and how the company's public positioning
differentiates from them. Only worth the time for final-round/exec-level
conversations where strategic framing is likely to come up directly.

**Check who the company actually competes with, don't assume it.** A
company can look like it competes with well-known names in its space when
it actually sells *to* them (supplier vs. rival) — verify the direction of
the relationship (client list, partnership announcements) before building
a competitive narrative on a wrong assumption.

**Adjacent-industry playbook translation (Deep only, high-value for
strategic/leadership rounds).** If the company's market has a more mature
analogue elsewhere (e.g. ad verification in digital vs. a physical/offline
channel; a SaaS category that already consolidated in an adjacent
vertical), research how the leaders in the mature analogue actually
grew — accreditation/certification milestones, what they integrated with
and in what order, how they bundled products to create lock-in, whether
they were acquired and by whom — then translate that sequence to the
target company's market. This produces a much sharper "here's the
market-penetration sequence I'd argue for" than positioning research
alone, and it's a strong smart-question source (Phase 4): naming the
playbook and asking whether they're consciously running it is a
genuinely high-value question, not a generic one.

## 6. Employer reputation & the existing team (Standard + Deep)

Two checks that senior candidates especially shouldn't skip — the
interview isn't only about the company assessing the candidate:
- **Employer/review sites** (Glassdoor, Comparably, Indeed reviews):
  patterns in why people leave, leadership reputation, anything that
  contextualizes *why this role is actually open* (a genuinely new
  function vs. backfilling a departure vs. a reorg). Treat individual
  reviews with the same skepticism as any anonymous source — look for a
  pattern across several, not one outlier — but a consistent theme is
  real signal a JD will never volunteer.
- **The existing team's LinkedIn profiles** (not just the named
  interviewers — see §2.5): scan the function this role will lead or
  join. Team size, seniority spread, tenure, and where people came from
  tells you the function's actual maturity far better than a headcount
  number in a recruiter brief — and shapes what "come in and build this"
  really means in practice (a green team needs different leadership than
  a seasoned one that's just missing a strategic head).

**Conference talks, podcast appearances, and webinars (Deep, or Standard
if quickly found)** — often more candid and detailed than press coverage,
and a good source for both company strategy signal and named-interviewer
research (§2.5) if a relevant exec has spoken publicly.

## Checkpoint

After research, present a tight summary (3-5 lines: what they do, how they
make money, most relevant recent news/strategic signal) before generating
questions and the intro — don't build Phase 3/4/5 on an unconfirmed read
if anything in the research was ambiguous or contradictory. In Rapid mode,
skip the checkpoint and proceed straight through — there's no time, and
Rapid mode already accepts a lower research bar.

**Also produce a short "verify before you say this out loud" list**
(Standard + Deep) — anything modeled, inferred, or not independently
confirmed (a funding/ownership assumption, a framework name the user
might describe differently than assumed, a maturity read on a competitor
gap). This goes into the dossier per `ip-dossier-template.md` so the user
walks in knowing exactly which of their own talking points are backed by
a source versus a best guess.

## If research is blocked

- Site/news behind a paywall or otherwise inaccessible: say so, proceed
  with what's available, and flag the gap rather than inventing detail.
- No public information at all (very early-stage/stealth): rely on the JD
  text itself, LinkedIn company page if accessible, and ask the user
  directly what they already know.
